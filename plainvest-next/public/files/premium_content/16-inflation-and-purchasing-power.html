What inflation means in plain English

Inflation is the gradual rise in the price of goods and services over time. When prices rise by 3% per year, a basket of items that costs $100 today will cost $103 next year. That sounds small. Over 20 years at 3%, that same basket costs approximately $180. Your $100 bill buys about 55 cents of what it used to.

The problem is not the number — it is the direction. Cash does not grow. It sits. And while it sits, prices around it move up. The result is a slow, invisible erosion of what that money can actually buy.

🏠
Housing and rent

Property prices and rents have historically risen faster than general CPI in Australia, the US, and Brazil — creating wealth for owners, cost pressure for renters.

🛒
Groceries and essentials

Food prices are volatile and often rise faster than headline inflation. The 2022–2023 inflation spike in all three countries was felt most sharply in supermarkets.

⚡
Energy and utilities

Electricity, gas, and fuel costs are sensitive to global commodity prices and infrastructure costs. Australian energy bills roughly doubled between 2021 and 2024.

🎓
Education and healthcare

These categories often rise faster than general CPI. US healthcare inflation has averaged 4–5% annually for decades. University costs in Australia have risen significantly above general inflation.

The silent tax: inflation is sometimes called a silent tax because it reduces the real value of money without any visible deduction. A 3% inflation rate means the government or central bank did not take anything from your account — but $10,000 in 2005 has the buying power of approximately $5,800 in 2025. The $4,200 difference was not taken. It was eroded.

Australia: the RBA, CPI, and what inflation has done here

Australia experienced low, stable inflation from the mid-1990s through 2021 — the RBA successfully kept CPI within its 2–3% target band for nearly three decades. Then, in 2022, a combination of post-COVID supply shocks, energy price increases, and strong domestic demand pushed Australian CPI to 7.8% — the highest in over 30 years.

RBA inflation target: 2–3%

The Reserve Bank of Australia uses the cash rate to influence inflation. When inflation rises, the RBA raises rates to slow spending. When it falls too low, rates are cut to stimulate. The 2022–2023 rate cycle saw the cash rate go from 0.10% to 4.35%.

What 7.8% inflation felt like

In the year to December 2022, the average Australian household experienced rapid increases in grocery prices, electricity bills, petrol, and rent — all at once. This is why understanding inflation is not academic for Australian investors.

Superannuation as inflation defence

Australia's super system — which invests contributions in equities, property, bonds, and infrastructure — has historically delivered returns above inflation over long periods. This is why your super is already acting as an inflation hedge, even if you have never thought of it that way.

Property and Australian inflation

Sydney and Melbourne median house prices have grown well above CPI over the past 30 years — in some periods, at 2–3× the rate of inflation. This is partly why home ownership has long been seen as an inflation hedge by Australians, even though it introduces its own risks and costs.

United States: the Fed, the 2022 shock, and what history shows

The United States Federal Reserve has a dual mandate: stable prices (approximately 2% inflation) and maximum employment. For most of the 2010s, US inflation ran below the 2% target. Then, in June 2022, US CPI hit 9.1% — the highest since 1981. The Fed responded with the fastest rate-hiking cycle in 40 years.

Fed inflation target: 2%

The Federal Reserve's 2% target is a long-run average, not a ceiling. Post-2022, the Fed has aimed to return inflation to that level without triggering a recession. By 2024–2025, US CPI had retreated to approximately 2.9%.

S&P 500 vs inflation: the long view

Over every 20-year rolling period since 1928, the S&P 500 has outpaced inflation in nominal terms. The real (inflation-adjusted) average annual return has been approximately 7%. This is the core case for broad equity index funds as a long-term wealth-preservation tool.

TIPS: the inflation-linked bond

Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) are US government bonds where the principal adjusts with CPI. They directly protect purchasing power, but offer lower yields than nominal bonds. Used by some investors as insurance in a diversified portfolio.

The 401(k) as inflation protection

Contributing to a 401(k) or Roth IRA invested in broad equity index funds creates a tax-advantaged inflation hedge. The combination of tax deferral and equity returns is one of the most powerful tools available to US investors — especially with employer matching.

The DALBAR implication: even though equities outpace inflation over the long run, the average US investor has consistently underperformed the index due to buying and selling at the wrong times. The tool (index fund) works. The behaviour (panic selling, chasing performance) is what destroys the inflation protection. This is why DCA and a written plan matter as much as choosing the right fund.

Brazil: a country that knows inflation the hard way

Brazil has the richest and most painful inflation history of the three countries covered by Plainvest. Brazilians who lived through the 1980s and 1990s experienced hyperinflation that at its peak reached over 6,000% per year. That era shaped Brazilian financial culture in ways that are still visible today — including very high fixed-income returns, a culture of diversification, and a deep-seated awareness that cash can become worthless quickly.

The hyperinflation period (1985–1994)

During Brazil's hyperinflation era, prices could double within weeks. Families bought groceries as soon as they received their pay, before prices rose. The government launched multiple currency plans (Cruzado, Cruzeiro, Real) before the Plano Real in 1994 finally stabilised the economy.

The Plano Real and stability

The Real was introduced in 1994 and brought inflation from thousands of percent down to single digits. The new currency held for decades and gave Brazil its modern investment infrastructure — Tesouro Direto, the B3, FIIs, and a regulated banking sector.

IPCA and the Banco Central target

Brazil's official inflation measure is IPCA (Índice de Preços ao Consumidor Amplo). The Banco Central do Brasil targets 3% ± 1.5%. As of 2025, IPCA is approximately 5.5% — above target, which is why the Selic rate remains elevated (~14.75%).

Why Brazilian fixed income pays so much

A CDB or Tesouro Selic paying 100% of CDI (approximately 14%+ gross) sounds extraordinary to an Australian or American investor. It is because Brazil's rates are set to contain inflation that runs hotter than Western economies. The nominal yield is high — but so is the inflation it is compensating for.

Tesouro IPCA+: the direct inflation hedge

Tesouro IPCA+ bonds pay the IPCA inflation rate plus a fixed real yield (e.g., IPCA+ 6%). This directly protects purchasing power — whatever inflation does, the principal adjusts. For Brazilian investors wanting to preserve real wealth over decades, Tesouro IPCA+ is one of the clearest tools available.

FIIs and real estate as inflation hedge

Brazilian Real Estate Investment Funds (FIIs) hold commercial property, logistics, shopping centres, and mortgage-backed securities. Rent contracts in Brazil are typically indexed to inflation measures (IPCA or IGP-M), which means distributions tend to keep pace with inflation over time.

What Brazilians understand that others are still learning: inflation is not theoretical. It changes what you can afford to eat, where you can afford to live, and how much your savings are worth next year. Brazil's financial culture — high savings rates, aggressive use of fixed income, early understanding of real vs nominal returns — is a direct product of having lived through extreme inflation. The rest of the world is catching up.

One year of waiting costs more than you think

Starting a $200/month DCA habit one year later means 12 fewer contributions. At 7%, those 12 contributions would have grown into approximately $6,000 in value over the full 20-year horizon. The opportunity cost of a single year's delay compounds over the entire investment period.

"I'll start when I know more"

Most beginner investors who delay for knowledge never reach the point where they feel ready. The market does not pause to let you finish studying. The knowledge that matters most — what you own, why you own it, what you will do when it falls — is available in this hub. You do not need more before your first contribution.

The perfect moment does not exist

Every year since the S&P 500 was created, there has been a reason not to invest: war, recession, inflation shock, banking crisis, pandemic. The investors who built wealth were not the ones who found the perfect entry. They were the ones who contributed consistently through the uncertainty.

Small amounts compound too

$50/month invested over 30 years at 7% grows to approximately $60,000 — from just $18,000 contributed. Waiting until you can invest a "meaningful" amount means losing the compounding on the small amounts that accumulate first. Start with what you have.

The real question is not "should I invest?" but "when do I start?" The answer inflation gives you is: as soon as you have your emergency fund covered, understand what you are buying, and have a platform set up. Every month you wait is a month where prices move up and your cash stays still.

Inflation by goal: which tools for which purpose

Different goals have different inflation sensitivities. A 6-month emergency fund and a 25-year retirement account should not be treated the same way, even though both involve money.

Emergency fund (3–6 months expenses)

Keep in HISA (AU), high-yield savings account (US), or Tesouro Selic (Brazil). Accept the inflation cost — this money's job is to be available, not to beat inflation. Do not invest your emergency fund in equities.

Short-term goals (1–3 years)

Term deposits, short-dated bonds, Tesouro Selic, TIPS, or bond ETFs. Inflation risk is real but manageable at these timeframes. Avoid equity exposure for money you need soon — short-term market drops can be severe.

Medium-term goals (3–7 years)

Mixed approach: partial equity (broad index), partial bond/fixed income. Time horizon is long enough to recover from moderate market drawdowns but short enough that 100% equity creates meaningful risk.

Long-term goals (7+ years)

Broad equity index funds have historically been the strongest inflation defence over this timeframe in all three countries. Superannuation (AU), Roth IRA / 401(k) (US), B3 ETFs and FIIs (Brazil) are the main vehicles. Time horizon makes short-term volatility tolerable.
