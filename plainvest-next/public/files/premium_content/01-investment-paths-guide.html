The hardest investment path is behaviour

The chart below is powerful because every red dot can feel like a strong reason to sell: recession fear, debt crisis, flash crash, political stress, oil shocks, war headlines, shutdowns, and bear-market panic. Yet over that period the broad market still climbed through repeated waves of fear.

This does not mean selling is always wrong. It means the market gives investors scary stories all the time, and a beginner without a written plan can confuse a headline with a strategy.

What studies suggest Behavioural-finance research points to three recurring problems: losses feel emotionally stronger than gains, frequent trading can reduce investor returns after costs, and panic decisions often happen when prices are already stressed.
Weak reasons to sell
  • A scary headline without a change in your original thesis.
  • Social media panic, predictions, or short-term market noise.
  • Trying to perfectly time the bottom or avoid every correction.
  • Selling only because the account is red and uncomfortable to look at.
Stronger reasons to sell
  • Your goal, time horizon, or cash need has genuinely changed.
  • The original investment thesis is broken or the risk is now too concentrated.
  • You are rebalancing back to a written plan.
  • Fees, tax, product structure, or platform risk no longer make sense.
Prospect theory

Kahneman and Tversky showed that people evaluate gains and losses differently under risk. In plain English, losing money can feel much more painful than the pleasure of making the same amount, which helps explain why drawdowns can trigger panic.

Overtrading evidence

Barber and Odean studied individual investor trading and found a clear pattern: more active trading was associated with lower net performance after costs. Activity can feel productive, but it is not the same as progress.

Plainvest rule

Before buying, write the sell rules. If you only decide after the market falls, emotion is already in the room. A simple rulebook protects you from turning volatility into a permanent mistake.

Index funds and ETFs

An index fund or ETF can hold hundreds or thousands of companies in one product. Instead of trying to pick the best company, you buy exposure to a market, sector, or index. This is why many beginner education sources place diversification near the center of long-term investing.

  • Check the index it tracks: S&P 500, Nasdaq-100, ASX 200, global shares, bonds, sectors, or themes.
  • Check the annual fee and trading cost. Small fee differences matter over decades.
  • Check whether the fund is broad or concentrated. A tech ETF and a total-market ETF are not the same risk.
  • Check currency exposure. A fund listed in your country can still own overseas assets.
Plain-English test: if you cannot explain what the fund owns, what fee it charges, and why you chose it, keep learning before buying.

Stocks, bonds, REITs, gold, and crypto

Individual stocks

You own a piece of one company. Upside can be high, but company-specific risk is real. Revenue, profit, debt, valuation, competition, and management matter.

Bonds and fixed income

Often used for income and stability. Interest rates, credit risk, maturity, inflation, and country rules matter.

REITs and FIIs

Property exposure without buying a full property. Watch interest rates, debt, tenant quality, property type, and payout sustainability.

Gold

Often used as a hedge against currency weakness or market fear. It does not pay income and can underperform growth assets for long periods.

Bitcoin

A scarce digital asset with a fixed issuance schedule. It is volatile, has deep drawdowns, and requires security knowledge.

Crypto assets

Very high risk. Projects can fail, platforms can collapse, scams are common, and custody mistakes can be permanent.

Decision checklist before going deeper

  1. Purpose: Is this for long-term growth, income, stability, inflation protection, or learning?
  2. Time horizon: Can you leave the money invested through bad markets?
  3. Risk: What could cause this to fall 20%, 50%, or more?
  4. Fees and tax: What are product fees, brokerage fees, spreads, and tax rules?
  5. Behaviour: Would you panic if the value dropped quickly?

Portfolio thinking: core and satellite

A professional way to reduce confusion is to separate a portfolio into core and satellite. The core is the stable learning foundation: broad diversified funds, long time horizon, recurring contributions, and low fees. Satellites are smaller positions used for learning, themes, higher risk, or specific beliefs.

Core examples

Global index ETF, S&P 500 ETF, ASX 200 ETF, diversified bond fund, cash buffer, broad retirement/superannuation allocation.

Satellite examples

Single stocks, sector ETFs, gold, Bitcoin, Ethereum, thematic ETFs, REITs/FIIs, or country-specific opportunities.

Member principle: the more concentrated, volatile, or complex an asset is, the smaller and more intentional it should be until your knowledge is stronger.

Investment policy statement: the professional habit

A simple investment policy statement is a written rulebook for your money. It reduces emotional decision-making because you decide the plan before the market tests you. Beginners can keep it one page.

Risk layers members should understand

Market risk

The investment falls because the whole market reprices. Diversification helps, but does not remove this risk.

Concentration risk

Too much money in one company, country, sector, platform, or narrative.

Liquidity risk

You cannot sell quickly at a fair price, or withdrawals are delayed.

Currency risk

Your asset may be priced in one currency while your life expenses are in another.

Behaviour risk

Panic selling, FOMO buying, overtrading, or changing strategy too often.

Platform risk

Broker, exchange, custody, account access, reporting, or withdrawal issues.

Rebalancing and review

A portfolio drifts over time. If one asset rises much faster than others, it can become a bigger part of your money than intended. Rebalancing is the habit of bringing the portfolio back toward the original plan.

Calendar review

Review every 6 or 12 months. This is simpler for beginners and avoids constant tinkering.

Threshold review

Review only when an allocation moves far from target, such as 5% or 10% away from the plan.

Contribution rebalance

Direct new contributions toward underweight assets before selling anything.

Tax-aware rebalance

Selling can create tax events, so members should understand local tax rules before changing positions.
