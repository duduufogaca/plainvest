Research workflow for any new investment idea

Follow these five questions in order. If you cannot answer one clearly, that is the gap to research before acting.

1
What is it?

Read the official issuer page, fund fact sheet, or asset description — not social media

→
2
What does it cost?

Expense ratio, brokerage, FX spread, tax treatment, and withdrawal conditions

→
3
What is the history?

TradingView monthly candles — full price history including worst drawdowns

→
4
What are the risks?

Write the two or three scenarios that could cause this to fall 50–80% or become worthless

→
5
Does it fit?

Core, satellite, watchlist, or avoid? Compare against your written investment plan

If you skip step 4: you have not done research — you have done confirmation. The risks section is the most important part of due diligence. Writing them down forces clarity about what you are accepting when you buy.

Investor education and portfolio foundations

ASIC Moneysmart

Australian Government source. Use it to understand ETFs, diversification, scam warnings, platform regulation checks, and investor rights. The managed funds and ETF sections are beginner-friendly.

Investor.gov (SEC)

US Government investor education. Use the compound interest calculator, fee comparison tool, and fund analyser. Good for understanding how small fee differences affect long-term outcomes.

Vanguard Investor Education

Vanguard's own education library covers goals, balance, cost, and discipline. Useful because Vanguard's index investing philosophy is evidence-based and conflict-free in the reading.

Bogleheads Wiki

Covers investment policy statements, asset allocation, index investing basics, tax-efficient fund placement, and long-term portfolio thinking. One of the most practical free resources for disciplined beginners.

ETF and fund research tools

ETF Database (etfdb.com)

Look up any ETF: expense ratio, holdings, sector breakdown, liquidity, dividend history, and performance. Use it to compare two similar ETFs before choosing one.

Morningstar Australia

Fund ratings, fee comparisons, ETF data, and analyst commentary. Good for comparing Australian ETFs and managed funds. Free data is sufficient for beginners.

ASX ETF Centre

Official list of ASX-listed ETFs. Check issuer, index tracked, management fee, and number of holdings for any ASX-listed fund before buying.

justETF Australia

ETF screener with side-by-side comparisons, TER (total expense ratio), replication method, and dividend policy. Useful when choosing between similar funds.

Bitcoin and crypto research tools

Mempool.space

Live Bitcoin block explorer and mempool visualiser. Use it to check block height, fee levels, transaction confirmation times, and the real state of the Bitcoin network without needing an account.

Glassnode Academy

On-chain analytics education. Learn what realized price, MVRV ratio, exchange balances, and long-term holder behaviour mean. Start with the free learning articles before interpreting the charts.

CoinGecko

Token data: market cap, volume, circulating supply, fully diluted valuation, and exchange listings. Compare these numbers for any token before deciding whether you understand it well enough to hold it.

Bitcoin White Paper

The original 9-page document. Read it slowly once without expecting to understand everything. It introduces the problem Bitcoin tries to solve better than any secondary explanation.

FTC Crypto Scam Guidance

US Federal Trade Commission guidance on recovery phrase scams, fake support, giveaway fraud, and impersonation. Read this before engaging with any person offering crypto help online.

Etherscan

Ethereum blockchain explorer. Check token contracts, wallet activity, smart contract code, and transaction history. If someone asks you to approve a contract, you can check what it does here first.

Crypto Fear & Greed Index — Alternative.me

Daily index (0–100) measuring overall crypto market sentiment from volatility, momentum, social volume, dominance, and search trends. Extreme fear has historically coincided with attractive entry points. Live data also appears on the Plainvest Sentiment page. Source used by Plainvest.

Chart reading and market context

TradingView

The main chart platform recommended in Guide 07. Start with a free account, set monthly candles, search any symbol (SPY, QQQ, BTCUSD, VAS, IVV, BOVA11, GOLD), and compare long-term price history before you buy anything.

FRED (St. Louis Fed)

Federal Reserve Economic Data. Charts for inflation (CPI), interest rates, money supply, unemployment, GDP, and more. Free, authoritative, and useful for understanding the macro context behind market moves.

Market sentiment tools

Sentiment indices measure whether investors are driven by fear or greed at a given moment. They are contrarian context tools — not buy or sell signals — and are most useful when read alongside price charts and your own written plan. Both indices below power the live gauges on the Plainvest Sentiment page.

CNN Fear & Greed Index — US Stock Market

Published by CNN Business. Scores US stock market sentiment from 0 (Extreme Fear) to 100 (Extreme Greed) using seven indicators: stock price momentum (S&P 500 vs its 125-day moving average), stock price strength (52-week highs vs lows on the NYSE), stock price breadth (McClellan Volume Summation Index), put and call options ratio, junk bond demand (yield spread), market volatility (VIX), and safe-haven demand (stocks vs bonds). Updated daily.

Raw API used by Plainvest: production.dataviz.cnn.io/index/fearandgreed/graphdata/

Crypto Fear & Greed Index — Alternative.me

Published by Alternative.me. Scores the overall crypto market sentiment from 0 (Extreme Fear) to 100 (Extreme Greed) using five weighted signals: volatility (25%) — compares current Bitcoin price against 30-day and 90-day averages; market momentum and volume (25%); social media volume and sentiment (15%); Bitcoin dominance (10%); and Google Trends data (10%). Updated daily. Historical data for the previous 30 days is available via the free API.

Free API used by Plainvest: api.alternative.me/fng/?limit=30

How to use both together: When the US stock market shows Extreme Fear but crypto shows Greed, there may be a divergence worth researching. When both show Extreme Fear simultaneously, it often marks a period of broader risk-off behaviour across markets. Neither reading tells you what to do — it tells you what the crowd is feeling. Your plan decides what to do with that information.

Macro, inflation, and monetary policy

Reserve Bank of Australia Education

Explains inflation, monetary policy, the cash rate, and how the RBA responds to economic conditions. Essential context for Australian investors trying to understand interest rate decisions.

Federal Reserve Education

Covers how the Fed sets rates, what inflation means, how money is created, and US monetary policy basics. Relevant for understanding US markets and global rate cycles that affect Australian assets.

Platform comparison: AU, US & Brazil

The right platform depends on your country of residence, the assets you want to buy, and how you plan to invest. These are the most commonly used options in each market, with the key factors that matter for a beginner investor.

Australia

Pearler

Built specifically for long-term ETF investors. Auto-invest DCA on a schedule, CHESS-sponsored holdings, flat $6.50 brokerage per ASX trade. No FX conversion needed for ASX assets. Best choice for systematic index investors who want automation without noise.

SelfWealth

Flat $9.50 per ASX trade regardless of size. CHESS-sponsored, meaning your holdings are registered in your name — not held by the broker as custodian. Good for buy-and-hold ETF investors who want clear, direct ownership.

Stake (AU)

Access to both ASX-listed and US-listed shares and ETFs from one account. Zero commission on ASX ETFs. Currency conversion fees apply when trading US stocks. Uses a custodian model rather than CHESS. Good for investors wanting US market access from Australia.

CommSec

Australia's largest retail broker, integrated with Commonwealth Bank. $5–$19.95 per trade depending on trade size. Strong education section and familiar interface. Good starting option for beginners who already bank with CBA and want simplicity over cost optimisation.

United States

Fidelity

Zero commissions on US stocks and ETFs. No account minimums. Fractional shares available. Strong research tools and education resources. SIPC-protected. Consistently rated one of the best platforms for long-term index investors.

Charles Schwab

Zero commissions on US stocks and ETFs. Acquired TD Ameritrade in 2020. Strong research tools, robo-advisor option (Schwab Intelligent Portfolios), and international desk for non-US residents. Good for long-term ETF investors who also want professional support options.

Interactive Brokers (IBKR)

Available to investors in over 200 countries. Best FX conversion rates among major brokers. Access to stocks, ETFs, options, futures, and bonds across global exchanges. More complex interface than beginner platforms — worth the learning curve for international investors or those needing global market access.

Brazil

XP Investimentos

Brazil's largest independent broker. Access to B3 stocks, ETFs, FIIs (real estate investment funds), BDRs (international stock receipts), Tesouro Direto, CDBs, LCIs, LCAs, and debentures. Strong research department and financial planning tools. Good for investors who want a broad product range.

Rico

Part of the XP group. Zero commission on stocks, ETFs, BDRs, and Tesouro Direto. Simple, beginner-friendly mobile app. Good starting point for investors on B3 who want zero-cost stock and ETF trading without complexity.

NuInvest (Nubank)

Part of the Nubank group. Zero commission on stocks, ETFs, and BDRs. Access to fixed income products and Tesouro Direto. Very clean, modern interface. Well-suited for investors who are already Nubank customers and want a unified app experience.

BTG Pactual Digital

Digital arm of Brazil's largest investment bank. Strong fixed income offering: CDBs, LCIs, LCAs, and debentures at competitive rates. Also covers stocks and FIIs. Better suited for investors with more capital who want access to institutional-grade products at a digital interface.

Key structural differences to understand: In Australia, CHESS sponsorship means your holdings are registered in your name with ASX — not held by the broker. In Brazil, IOF tax applies to foreign currency transactions, which affects the real cost of investing in BDRs or US assets. In the US, the Pattern Day Trader rule applies if you make four or more day trades in five business days with under $25,000 in your account — not relevant for long-term investors but worth knowing.

Sources to treat with caution

Not all investing information is equally useful. Some sources are designed to drive action, not inform decisions. Recognising these patterns is part of research discipline — knowing what to ignore is as important as knowing where to look.

Source typeWhy to be cautiousBetter question to ask
YouTube price predictions ("BTC to $500k by Q2")Views and engagement reward dramatic claims. Wrong predictions are rarely reviewed. Creators rarely disclose their own positions or track record.Is this person explaining their reasoning, or just naming a dramatic number?
Anonymous Telegram and Discord "alpha" groupsGroup owners may hold positions they want others to buy and pump. Urgency and exclusivity are emotional pressure tactics, not analysis.Who is behind this group, and what do they gain from sharing this information for free?
Paid signal servicesA service charging for buy/sell signals profits from subscriptions, not from your results. If the signals worked reliably, why sell them instead of using them personally?Where is the independently audited track record?
Social media community consensus on tokensPopularity on Twitter/X, Reddit, or TikTok does not reflect underlying quality. Large communities can form around structurally bad projects.What is the on-chain activity? What is the FDV vs market cap? Who funded development?
Influencer affiliate and sponsored contentPlatform referral codes and sponsored posts create financial incentives to recommend specific products regardless of suitability. Disclosure is often minimal.Is this person disclosing a financial relationship with the product or platform?
Exchange-produced "research" and token listingsAn exchange that lists a token has a direct business interest in trading volume. Their analysis may frame risk differently than an independent source.Who funded this analysis, and does the publisher profit if people buy this asset?
The incentive test: if this information turns out to be wrong, who loses — the source, or you? Government bodies, independent analysts, and white-paper authors have different incentive structures than content creators, signal sellers, and exchange marketing departments. Use this as your filter.

How to use sources well

Read the original, not the summary

ATO guidance, white papers, fund fact sheets, and official product documents carry more weight than blog posts or social summaries of the same information.

Ask: who published this and why?

Government sources, academic research, and regulated issuers have different incentives than influencers, token promoters, or commission-based product comparisons.

Use sources to build questions, not just confirm beliefs

Good research reveals complexity and risk, not only support for something you already want to do.

Bring verified questions to support

The Zoom call is most useful when you have already done basic research and you have a specific question about something that still feels unclear.

Australian tax and regulation tools

ATO Crypto Guidance

Official ATO guidance on crypto as a capital asset. Explains CGT events, disposal rules, record-keeping, and how staking rewards are treated. Read this before lodging any tax return that includes crypto.

CryptoTaxCalculator (Australian)

Australian-built crypto tax software. Imports transaction history from most exchanges, calculates CGT, and produces tax reports compatible with Australian accountants. Useful if you have more than a handful of transactions.

ATO Super Comparison Tool

Compares Australian super funds by fees, investment returns, insurance costs, and performance. Use it with the Superannuation guide to identify whether your current fund competes with alternatives.

Moneysmart: Tax and Investments

ASIC's plain-English explanation of how tax applies to shares, ETFs, dividends, and capital gains. Good as a first read before speaking to an accountant.

Australian tax and CGT basics

This section covers how tax works for Australian investors holding shares, ETFs, and crypto. It is educational context — not tax advice. Always verify with the ATO or a registered tax agent for your specific situation.

What is Capital Gains Tax (CGT)?

CGT is not a separate tax — it is part of your income tax. When you sell, swap, or gift an investment asset, the gain (disposal price minus cost base) is added to your taxable income for that financial year. The more you earn, the higher the rate at which the gain is taxed.

The 12-month CGT discount

If you hold an asset for more than 12 months before disposal, Australian individuals receive a 50% discount on the taxable gain. A $10,000 gain on a long-held ETF becomes $5,000 of taxable income. This is one of the most powerful structural incentives for patient, long-term investing in Australia.

What triggers a CGT event?

Selling shares or ETFs. Selling, swapping, or spending crypto (including swapping BTC for ETH — this is a disposal of BTC). Gifting an asset. Receiving tokens from a fork or airdrop may also trigger an income event. Buying with AUD is not a CGT event. Transferring between your own wallets is not a CGT event.

Cost base: what it includes

Your cost base includes the purchase price plus directly related costs: brokerage fees on purchase and sale, foreign exchange conversion costs, and some capital improvement costs. Getting this right matters because it determines how large your taxable gain actually is. Keep records of every cost attached to each buy and sell.

Record keeping requirements

The ATO requires you to keep records of every acquisition and disposal for at least five years after the CGT event. For each trade: date, price, number of units, and brokerage cost. For crypto: exchange records, wallet addresses, and timestamps. Most good brokers and exchanges allow you to export full history as CSV.

Financial year and lodgement

The Australian financial year runs 1 July to 30 June. Tax returns are generally due by 31 October unless you use a registered tax agent, who can extend this. All CGT events from 1 July to 30 June are reported in that year's return. Losses from one asset can offset gains from another in the same year.

Tax-loss harvesting

If you have unrealised losses, you can sell to realise the loss and offset it against gains. The loss reduces your taxable income. You can repurchase the same asset immediately — there is no wash-sale rule in Australia as there is in the US. Be mindful of brokerage costs and whether the tax saving justifies the transaction.

Franking credits (dividends)

Australian companies pay tax at 30% before distributing dividends. Shareholders receive a "franking credit" representing that tax already paid. If your marginal tax rate is lower than 30%, you can use the credit as a tax offset — or receive a refund. This is called dividend imputation and is unique to Australia.

Before tax time: export your full transaction history from every platform and exchange you used during the financial year. For crypto with many transactions, a tool like CryptoTaxCalculator (above) reduces the risk of error. For complex situations — multiple assets, overseas accounts, or crypto staking — consult a registered tax agent who handles investment portfolios.

Superannuation basics

Superannuation is Australia's compulsory retirement savings system. It is one of the most tax-efficient investment structures available to Australians — but most people leave it in a default fund without ever reviewing it. Understanding the basics helps you make better decisions about contributions, fund choice, and investment options inside super.

What super is

A concessional (tax-advantaged) savings structure designed to fund retirement. Your employer is legally required to contribute 11.5% of your ordinary time earnings into a super fund (the Superannuation Guarantee, rising to 12% from July 2025). These contributions, and the earnings inside super, are taxed at a flat 15% — well below most people's marginal income tax rate.

Investment options inside super

Most funds offer pre-mixed options — Conservative, Balanced, Growth, High Growth — based on how much of the fund is allocated to shares vs bonds and cash. Your default is usually Balanced. If you are under 50 with a long investment horizon, a High Growth option (90%+ in shares) typically outperforms Balanced over 20+ years. Check your fund's option fact sheets before switching.

Concessional contributions

Money going into super before income tax — employer contributions plus any salary sacrifice you add. Taxed at 15% inside the fund rather than your marginal rate (which may be 32.5%, 37%, or 45%). The annual concessional cap is $30,000 (2024–25). Unused concessional cap from prior years can be carried forward for up to five years if your super balance is under $500,000.

Non-concessional contributions

After-tax money added voluntarily. No tax going in (you've already paid income tax on it). Earnings inside super are still taxed at 15%. Annual cap is $120,000 (2024–25). The bring-forward rule allows individuals under 75 to contribute up to $360,000 in a single year if they trigger it. Not available if your total super balance exceeds $1.9 million.

Choosing a super fund

Compare using total fees (administration + investment fees as a percentage of balance), net return after fees over 5–10 years, insurance premiums included in the default, and the investment options available. The ATO Super Comparison Tool and APRA's MySuper Heatmap are free official tools. Industry super funds have historically outperformed retail funds after fees on average.

First Home Super Saver Scheme (FHSS)

Allows eligible first-home buyers to make voluntary contributions into super and later withdraw up to $50,000 (lifetime) for a first home deposit. Contributions are taxed at 15% going in rather than your marginal rate — the saving can be meaningful. Withdrawals are taxed at your marginal rate minus a 30% offset. Applications go through the ATO. Plan well in advance of needing the funds.

Accessing super (preservation)

Super is preserved until you meet a condition of release. The most common is reaching your preservation age (60 for anyone born after June 1964) and retiring, or reaching 65 regardless of work status. Accessing super early without a valid condition is illegal and carries severe penalties. Valid exceptions include permanent incapacity, terminal illness, or severe financial hardship — these require ATO approval.

Self-Managed Super Fund (SMSF)

An SMSF lets up to six members (typically family) manage their own super. It can hold assets like direct shares, ETFs, property, and even Bitcoin (if the trust deed allows). Benefits: control and flexibility. Costs: setup fees ($1,500–$3,000+), ongoing accounting, auditing obligations, and ATO compliance. Generally not cost-effective unless the combined balance exceeds $300,000–$500,000.

Simple action this week: log in to your super fund (or find it via ATO MyGov if you've lost track), check which investment option your money is currently in, and compare the fee percentage. If you haven't reviewed your super in the last two years, it is worth 20 minutes. Use the ATO Super Comparison Tool linked in the section above.
