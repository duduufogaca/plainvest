Premium Research Guide

Read charts without becoming a gambler.

Chart tools can help members understand price history, trend, volatility, and levels. They should support research, not replace a plan.

What charts are useful for

Context

See whether an asset is near highs, lows, or a long sideways range.

Volatility

Understand how much an asset can move before you own it.

Planning

Set alerts, watchlists, and research notes before emotions are high.

Risk

Recognize when a move is already extended and your behaviour may be driven by FOMO.

Beginner-safe chart process

  1. Start with the weekly chart to understand the big picture.
  2. Look at drawdowns from previous highs. Could you handle that emotionally?
  3. Compare price to simple moving averages only as context, not as a magic signal.
  4. Add alerts instead of staring at screens every day.
  5. Write your reason before buying. If the reason changes after price moves, pause.

Chart concepts members should know

ConceptPlain meaningHow to use it safely
TrendThe broad direction over a chosen timeframe.Start weekly/monthly before short-term charts.
VolumeHow much activity happened during a move.Use as context, not proof that price must continue.
Support/resistanceAreas where buyers or sellers previously appeared.Treat as zones, not exact magic lines.
Moving averagesSmoothed price over time.Useful for context, dangerous as blind buy/sell signals.
DrawdownHow far price fell from a previous high.Use it to test emotional readiness before investing.

Research workflow

  1. Read the asset description and know what it actually is.
  2. Check long-term chart history, not only the recent move.
  3. Check fees, liquidity, custody, and tax considerations.
  4. Write a one-paragraph thesis and a one-paragraph risk case.
  5. Only then decide whether it belongs in core, satellite, watchlist, or avoid.

What to avoid

A chart can make risky behaviour feel professional. Avoid leverage, revenge trading, copying social media entries, and believing that indicators can predict the future with certainty. Long-term wealth usually comes from process, patience, and position sizing, not constant trading.

Multi-timeframe thinking

Many beginners make mistakes because they look at a short-term chart while making a long-term decision. The timeframe should match the goal.

TimeframeUseful forDanger
MonthlyLong-term trend, major cycles, large drawdowns.Too slow for short-term decisions.
WeeklyCleaner investor context without daily noise.Still not a prediction tool.
DailyEntry context, volatility, alerts, support/resistance zones.Can trigger overchecking and emotional decisions.
IntradayMostly trading, not long-term investing.High noise, high emotion, easy overtrading.

Indicators beginners commonly misuse

Most beginner chart mistakes do not come from not knowing enough indicators — they come from using simple indicators as if they make investing decisions obvious. They do not.

IndicatorWhat it actually doesCommon misuse
RSI (Relative Strength Index)Measures recent price speed on a 0–100 scale. Above 70 is called "overbought", below 30 "oversold".Treating 70+ as a sell signal and 30- as a buy signal. An asset can stay "overbought" for months during a trend.
MACDShows momentum by comparing two moving averages. Crossovers are often highlighted.Treating every crossover as an entry or exit. Most crossovers produce false signals in choppy markets.
Bollinger BandsPrice channels showing standard deviation from a moving average.Assuming price must "bounce" from the lower band or "reverse" from the upper. Price can walk the band for extended periods.
Moving average crossovers"Golden cross" (50-day crosses above 200-day) is often described as bullish.The cross lags actual price — it often appears after the move has already happened.
Fibonacci levelsPrice retracement levels drawn at specific percentages between a high and low.Treating them as precise support/resistance. They are zones, and price does not respect them reliably.
Plainvest rule: Indicators explain past price. They do not predict future price. Use them to understand context and volatility, not to generate "signals" that tell you when to buy or sell.

Economic events that move markets

Markets do not move randomly. Certain scheduled announcements regularly create price volatility across stocks, ETFs, Bitcoin, and currencies. Knowing when these happen helps members avoid being surprised, and helps avoid placing orders immediately before major events.

EventWho publishes itWhy it moves marketsHow often
Central bank interest rate decisionUS Fed, RBA, ECB, othersRate changes affect borrowing costs, stock valuations, and currency. Unexpected changes can cause sharp moves in all assets.8 times/year (US Fed); 8 times/year (RBA)
US CPI inflation reportUS Bureau of Labor StatisticsShows whether inflation is rising or falling. Affects expectations for Fed rate decisions and growth stocks heavily.Monthly
Australian CPIAustralian Bureau of Statistics (ABS)Affects RBA rate expectations, property-linked assets, and consumer confidence.Quarterly (monthly from 2023)
US Non-Farm Payrolls (jobs report)US Bureau of Labor StatisticsStrong jobs = Fed may raise rates. Weak jobs = potential cuts. Major US market mover on release day.First Friday of each month
Company earnings (ASX / S&P 500)Individual companiesRevenue, profit, and guidance can move individual stocks 10–30% on a single day. ETFs are less affected unless a giant like Apple or BHP reports.Quarterly (US); Semi-annual (ASX)
US GDP reportBureau of Economic AnalysisMeasures overall economic output. Two consecutive negative quarters = technical recession definition. Moves broad markets.Quarterly (advance, revised, final)
FOMC meeting minutesUS Federal ReserveMore detail on what Fed members discussed. Can shift bond and currency markets significantly.3 weeks after each meeting
Bitcoin halvingBitcoin protocol (block schedule)Reduces new issuance. Historically associated with narratives around scarcity. Not a guaranteed price catalyst.Approximately every 4 years
Practical use: add key economic dates to a calendar. Avoid placing large limit orders or making major buying decisions on the same day as a major Fed meeting, CPI print, or company earnings. Prices can gap significantly in either direction within minutes of the announcement.

Reading a candlestick: the basics every investor should know

Candlestick charts show four prices for each time period: open, high, low, and close. Most serious investors use candlestick charts rather than line charts because they show more information at a glance. You do not need to memorise dozens of patterns — but understanding one candle is genuinely useful.

What to read from a single candle

A green (or white) candle means price closed higher than it opened — buyers were dominant. A red (or black) candle means price closed lower than it opened — sellers were dominant. The wick shows how far price traveled outside the body: a long upper wick means price was rejected at higher levels. A long lower wick means buyers stepped in after a drop.

What not to read from a single candle

A single candle does not predict the next one. One big red candle after months of green candles can be noise, a news event, or the start of a reversal — you cannot tell from one candle alone. Context (where is this on the monthly chart? what just happened macroeconomically?) matters far more than the candle pattern in isolation.

Candle featureWhat it can suggestWhat to check alongside it
Long green body, small wicksStrong buying pressure throughout the period — bulls were in control.Is this consistent with the trend, or a single spike in a bearish context?
Long red body, small wicksStrong selling pressure — bears were in control throughout.Is this the 5th consecutive red candle (trend) or the 1st after a big rise (potential reversal or news)?
Long upper wick, small bodyPrice pushed higher but was rejected — sellers entered near the highs.Did this happen near a historically significant resistance area?
Long lower wick, small body (called a hammer)Price dropped sharply but recovered — buyers emerged near the lows.Is this near a support area? Does volume confirm that buyers were active?
Doji (open ≈ close, both wicks)Indecision — neither buyers nor sellers dominated the period.After a strong trend, a doji may suggest the trend is weakening. In a range, it may mean nothing.
Plainvest candlestick rule: use candles to understand what already happened, not to predict what must happen next. A hammer candle at a support area on a weekly chart is interesting context for research. It is not a buy signal.
