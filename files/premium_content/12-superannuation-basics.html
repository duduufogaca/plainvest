Australia Basics

Your super is already investing.

Superannuation is often the largest investment Australians will ever own. This guide helps beginners understand what to check, what not to ignore, and why small differences compound for decades.

Core ideaYour fund invests on your behalf.
Big leversFees, option, insurance, contributions.
MindsetSmall settings can matter over decades.

What super is

Super is a retirement account. Your employer contributes money, your fund invests it, and the account is generally locked until retirement age. Even if you have never bought an ETF yourself, your super means you are already an investor. The question is whether you understand how your fund is investing for you.

Beginner reframe: Super is not a boring payslip line. It is your long-term investment engine.

What to compare

Fees

Admin fees, investment fees, insurance premiums, and indirect costs can quietly reduce long-term returns.

Investment option

MySuper default, balanced, growth, high growth, indexed, ethical, or DIY-style options can behave very differently.

Performance

Compare long-term returns after fees, not only the last 12 months. One good year does not prove a strong fund.

Insurance

Check whether default insurance is useful, duplicated, too expensive, or not needed for your situation.

Contributions: the beginner map

Super contributions can be powerful, but they interact with tax, contribution caps, income, age, and goals. Learn the labels before adding extra money.

Employer contributions

Required contributions paid by your employer. Check they are arriving and going to the right fund.

Salary sacrifice

Pre-tax contributions arranged through your employer. Can be tax-effective for some people, but caps matter.

Personal concessional contributions

Personal contributions where you may claim a tax deduction. Requires correct notice process.

Non-concessional contributions

After-tax contributions. Useful in some situations, but not automatically right for beginners.

The Superannuation Guarantee rate

Your employer is legally required to contribute a percentage of your ordinary time earnings to your super fund. This is called the Superannuation Guarantee (SG). The rate changes on a schedule set by law.

Financial yearSG ratePractical meaning
2024–2511.5%Your employer must pay 11.5 cents for every dollar of ordinary earnings into super.
2025–26 and beyond12.0%The rate reaches its legislated target of 12% from 1 July 2025.
Check your payslip: If employer contributions are not appearing in your fund within 28 days of each quarter, you can report a non-payment to the ATO. Underpayment is more common than most employees expect.

Find all your super accounts

Many Australians have multiple super accounts from different jobs, often without realising. Multiple accounts mean multiple sets of fees and insurance premiums quietly reducing your balance. Finding and consolidating them is one of the highest-impact actions a beginner can take.

  1. Log in to MyGov at my.gov.au and link the ATO service if not already done.
  2. Go to Super → Information → Manage. The ATO shows all super accounts linked to your Tax File Number.
  3. Note each fund's name, balance, and fees. Write them down before deciding which to keep.
  4. Compare using the ATO Super Comparison Tool at ato.gov.au before consolidating.
  5. Check insurance before closing an account. Some insurance attached to an old fund may not transfer or be available at the same terms in a new one.
  6. Consolidate through MyGov or your chosen fund. The process takes a few days and the old account closes automatically once balance transfers.

Preservation age and access

Super is designed for retirement, so it is locked away from most people until they reach a qualifying age and meet a condition of release. Understanding this prevents surprises when financial planning later.

Key point: For most people born after 1 July 1964 — which includes most current workers — you cannot access super until age 60, and only under a qualifying condition such as retirement. Super should not be considered accessible money for short-term goals.

First Home Super Saver Scheme (FHSS)

The FHSS allows eligible first home buyers to make voluntary super contributions and then withdraw them (plus earnings) to use as a home deposit. It is one of the few ways to access super before preservation age for a defined purpose.

How it works

You make extra voluntary contributions to super (concessional or non-concessional). Later, you apply to the ATO to release up to $15,000 per financial year, to a lifetime maximum of $50,000, for a first home deposit.

Tax advantage

Concessional contributions go in at 15% tax instead of your marginal rate. Withdrawals are taxed at your marginal rate minus a 30% offset, which can be better than saving in a regular account.

Key conditions

You must never have owned property in Australia, you must intend to live in the property you buy, and you must apply for the FHSS determination before signing a contract.

Important caution

FHSS rules are detailed. Contribution caps, fund rules, eligibility requirements, and timing matter. Get qualified advice before relying on this scheme.

Super comparison tools

ATO Super Comparison Tool

Official government tool. Compares fees, net returns, insurance costs, and investment performance across regulated funds. Use it before changing funds or consolidating.

Moneysmart Super Comparison

ASIC's guided comparison framework. Explains what fees to look for, how to read performance data, and what questions to ask a fund before switching.

Beginner checklist

  • Log in to MyGov and check how many super accounts exist under your TFN.
  • Know your current fund, balance, investment option, fees, and insurance cost.
  • Compare long-term net performance (after fees) against alternatives using official comparison tools.
  • Check whether default insurance is still appropriate for your situation.
  • Understand contribution caps before adding voluntary contributions.
  • If you may buy your first home, research the FHSS scheme with a tax professional before acting.
  • Ask a professional if you are unsure about tax, caps, fund changes, or personal suitability.

Do not rush changes

Super is powerful because it compounds for decades, but it is also personal. Tax, contribution caps, age, income, insurance, family situation, and retirement goals matter. Use this guide to ask better questions, then get qualified advice before making major super decisions.
