Understand Bitcoin before buying Bitcoin.

Why it was createdPor que foi criado

Bitcoin was released after the 2008 financial crisis as a peer-to-peer electronic cash system that does not require a central bank or payment company to settle transactions. Its design combines a public ledger, proof-of-work mining, fixed issuance rules, and user-controlled keys.

Plain-English framing:Explicação simples: Bitcoin is not just “price going up or down.” It is a monetary network with programmed supply, open settlement, and a high responsibility custody model.

Purchasing power: the professional way to think about itPoder de compra: a forma profissional de pensar sobre isso

Bitcoin is often discussed through purchasing-power examples because the core question is not only “what is the price?” but “what does money buy over long periods?” This visual is illustrative, not a promise: it helps members compare a scarce digital asset narrative with currencies that can lose purchasing power over time.

What this example is sayingO que este exemplo está dizendo

What it does not proveO que isso não prova

It does not guarantee Bitcoin will rise, replace property, or protect every investor. It is a conversation starter about scarcity, purchasing power, volatility, and time horizon.

The halving schedule is fixed into Bitcoin's code. Every 210,000 blocks (approximately four years), the subsidy miners receive per block is cut in half. Halving and supplyHalving e oferta

Bitcoin’s issuance schedule reduces the new bitcoin subsidy roughly every four years. This event is called a halving. The fixed supply narrative matters because it contrasts with money systems where supply can expand by policy decision. Scarcity alone does not guarantee price, but it is central to Bitcoin’s design.

Bitcoin ETFs: owning exposure without self-custodyETFs de Bitcoin: ter exposição sem autocustódia

In January 2024, the US Securities and Exchange Commission approved spot Bitcoin ETFs. This created a new way for investors to get Bitcoin price exposure through regulated financial products, without managing private keys or exchanges directly.

What a Bitcoin ETF is

A fund that holds actual Bitcoin (spot ETF) or Bitcoin futures contracts (futures ETF). Shares trade on stock exchanges like NASDAQ or NYSE. You buy shares through your brokerage account — just like an index fund. The fund custodian holds the Bitcoin, not you.

Major spot Bitcoin ETFs (US)

iShares Bitcoin Trust (IBIT) by BlackRock — the largest by assets. Fidelity Wise Origin Bitcoin Fund (FBTC). ARK 21Shares Bitcoin ETF (ARKB). Bitwise Bitcoin ETF (BITB). Each has a management fee (typically 0.12%–0.25%). Check the most recent data for current AUM and fees.

Advantages of Bitcoin ETFs

No custody responsibility — no seed phrases, no hardware wallets. Accessible through existing brokerage accounts, including retirement accounts (IRA, 401k). Familiar tax reporting via standard brokerage statements. Regulated and insured at the custodial level.

Disadvantages and trade-offs

Annual management fee reduces returns compared to direct holding. You do not control the keys — platform/custodian risk replaces personal custody risk. Cannot use Bitcoin for payments or DeFi. Not available in all countries — check local regulation. Bitcoin in a Roth IRA grows tax-free, but ETF adds a small ongoing cost vs direct.

Australia: Bitcoin ETFs on ASX

ASX-listed Bitcoin ETFs include VanEck Bitcoin ETF (VBTC) and Global X 21Shares Bitcoin ETF (EBTC). These allow Australian investors to access Bitcoin exposure within their brokerage account, subject to ASX trading hours. Management fees apply. Not available inside superannuation in most standard funds.

ETF vs direct custody: the real trade-off

ETF: simpler, familiar infrastructure, no custody risk, works in retirement accounts, small annual fee. Direct custody: full ownership, no counterparty risk if self-custodied correctly, zero ongoing fee, but requires understanding of seed phrases, wallets, and security. Neither is wrong — the right choice depends on your knowledge level, account type, and risk tolerance.

For beginners: if you want Bitcoin exposure without learning self-custody first, a regulated ETF in a brokerage account is a lower-risk starting point. If you want to own Bitcoin directly and understand the custody responsibility, the hardware wallet and seed phrase path is the deeper option. You can do both — ETF first, self-custody study alongside.

Bitcoin vs other cryptoBitcoin vs outras criptomoedas

Members should avoid treating all crypto assets as the same. Bitcoin’s thesis is usually about monetary scarcity, decentralised settlement, censorship resistance, and long-term store-of-value debate. Many other crypto assets are closer to software networks, startup-like speculation, governance tokens, or applications with different risks.

Bitcoin questions

Can the supply schedule remain credible? Is self-custody understood? How does volatility fit your risk tolerance?

Altcoin questions

Who controls development? What are token unlocks? Is there real demand? Can insiders sell into retail liquidity?

Member Bitcoin checklist

  • Read the white paper summary before focusing on price predictions.
  • Understand private keys, addresses, transaction finality, and wallet backups.
  • Study previous drawdowns so volatility is not a surprise.
  • Learn the difference between owning Bitcoin and owning exposure through an ETF or platform.
  • Never use leverage as a beginner.

Bitcoin allocation thinking

Before deciding any allocation, members should separate belief from sizing. A person can believe Bitcoin is important and still choose a small position because volatility, custody responsibility, and personal finances matter.
