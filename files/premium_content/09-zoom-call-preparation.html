Best topics for the call

Investment basics

Ask about ETFs, index funds, bonds, fixed income, gold, Bitcoin, crypto, or how to compare paths before choosing.

Exchange setup

Ask about platform choice, account setup, fees, security settings, deposits, orders, and how to track your trades.

Learning plan

Crypto safety

Ask about wallets, self-custody, seed phrases, hardware devices, withdrawal tests, and scam warning signs.

Bring your written investment plan (even one paragraph) and ask what gaps, risks, or questions you should address before acting.

The week before your call

  1. Read the guide most relevant to your main question. If you want to talk about platforms, re-read the Exchange Checklist first. If it's Bitcoin, read the Bitcoin Research section. Arriving with guide knowledge means you can skip basics and get to your specific question.
  2. Write your three most important questions. Not topics — actual questions. "ETF fees" is a topic. "Is the 0.07% expense ratio on DHHF vs 0.18% on a competitor ETF meaningful on a $10,000 account over 20 years?" is a question.
  3. Write your context. What country you are in. What platform(s) you have opened or are considering. What you have already invested in or plan to. What you have already read or studied.
  4. Prepare any screenshots or account details you want to show. Remove personal numbers, balances, and identity details before screen-sharing. The focus should be on the platform mechanics, not your private financial position.
  5. Write one sentence about what a successful call looks like for you. "I want to leave knowing which platform to open and what to set up in security settings." That sentence focuses the whole 60 minutes.

What makes a question worth 60 minutes

Good questions are specific

"I am looking at X, which does Y, and I am confused about Z" works far better than "tell me about ETFs." The more specific the question, the more useful the answer.

Good questions include your context

Your country, the platforms you use, what you have already studied, and what you plan to do — these details change the answer completely. Two people asking about Bitcoin have very different needs if one is Australian and one is Brazilian.

Good questions have a decision at the end

The best questions lead to something: "So I can decide which platform to open." "So I know whether my DCA amount and ETF choice make sense for my timeline." The call is most useful when it removes a specific confusion that has been preventing action.

Avoid "tell me everything about X"

Open-ended requests fill the time with general education rather than specific help. You can read general education in the guides. Save the call for the questions the guides don't fully answer for your situation.

Prepare before the meeting

  2. Write what country you live in and which platform you are considering.

After the call

  • Write the three clearest takeaways while they are fresh.
  • Choose one next action: read, compare, set up security, or keep learning.
  • Do not make a financial decision during emotional excitement right after the call.
  • Save new questions for support or a future extra call if needed.

Boundaries

The call is educational support. It is not personal financial advice, tax advice, legal advice, or a promise of investment results. The purpose is to help you understand options, risks, tools, and next questions more clearly.
